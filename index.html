The real epiphany: it's not the 403(b), it's the brokerage rate
Max the 403(b)/401(k) and fund a Roth IRA every year — that part is basically automatic and capped by law. Most hospitals also offer a 457(b), another $24,500 pre-tax that most people never use. At PA and NP tax brackets (often 22–24% federal), a Roth 403(b) is worth considering for part of your contribution if your plan offers one — same limit, tax paid now instead of in retirement. But the single biggest lever, the one actually within your control every single year, is your taxable brokerage savings rate. Tax-advantaged accounts are capped. The taxable brokerage does not. Pay yourself first, monthly, on autopilot — treat the brokerage transfer like a bill that's due before lifestyle spending happens, and raise it every time pay increases or a big fixed cost ends. When daycare ends, when loans are paid off — redirect that exact amount the same month, before it gets absorbed.

Lifestyle creep and the 12-month rule
Lifestyle creep doesn't feel like a decision — it feels like finally affording normal things. It is the main reason well-paid clinicians aren't wealthy. The 12-month rule: when income rises (your first real paycheck, a raise, a bonus, a cost that ends), keep spending flat for 12 months and send the difference to savings. After a year you won't miss what you never started spending, and the higher savings rate feels normal. Made once at the jump from student to first paycheck, this decision is worth more than any investment choice you'll ever make.
Plan for the expensive years you can see coming: parental leave (often partly unpaid), fertility treatment, a new car, a wedding. Fund them from a separate savings bucket so they don't come out of the brokerage transfer.
Size fixed costs to guaranteed pay. Bonuses can shrink or vanish with reimbursement cuts, a bad year, or a job change. The mortgage and tuition don't. If the house only works with the bonus, it's too much house.

        How to invest it — the three-fund portfolio
        Keep it simple. You don't need to pick stocks, time the market, or pay anyone to do it for you. Own the whole market, cheaply, and leave it alone.
        • Three low-cost index funds: a US total stock market fund, an international total stock market fund, and a US total bond market fund. That's the whole portfolio.
        • Mostly stocks while you're young — they're what grows. Add bonds gradually as retirement gets closer, so a crash late in your career can't derail you.
        • Keep costs low. Total-market index funds at Vanguard, Fidelity and Schwab cost a few hundredths of a percent a year.
        • Automate it — the same funds, every month, in every account.
        • Rebalance once a year back to your mix. Ignore the news the rest of the year.
        • Never sell in a crash. Crashes are normal; selling is what makes them permanent.
        • Skip the 1% advisor. On $2M that's $20,000 a year, forever. If you want help, pay a flat fee.
        To go deeper: The Simple Path to Wealth (JL Collins) and the Bogleheads wiki's "Three-fund portfolio" page.

        The simple math — how much you need, and why your savings rate matters most
        1. How much do I need? The 4% rule
        1. Where it comes from. In the 1990s, researchers tested every historical stretch of the US market — including the Great Depression and the inflation of the 1970s — to see how much retirees could safely spend.
        2. What it says. Take 4% of your portfolio in your first year of retirement, raise that dollar amount with inflation every year after, and historically the money lasted at least 30 years.
        3. The arithmetic. Divide what you'll spend each year by 4% — the same as multiplying by 25.
        4. The fine print. Retiring before about 55 means a longer retirement, so a more cautious 3.5% (×28.6) is reasonable: . Social Security at 70 isn't counted and adds a cushion later.
        2. Why your savings rate matters more than your salary
        Your savings rate works on both ends at once. Every dollar you save is a dollar invested and a dollar you've learned to live without — so the number you need shrinks too. Salary helps, but only through what you save: a raise that turns into a bigger house and a nicer car raises your number as fast as your savings.         When does work become optional?

        Work optional = saved enough that you could stop saving and still retire on time. From then on your paycheck only has to cover today's bills — so you can leave a bad job, go part-time, or drop nights and weekends. Some people call it FU money.

        What changed in 2026
        SAVE — eliminated. Struck down by federal courts. Borrowers moved to IBR or interest-only forbearance. No reinstatement expected.
        RAP — new July 1, 2026. Repayment Assistance Plan. 1% of total AGI at $10–20k, rising 1 point per $10k bracket to 10% above $100k; minus $50/month per dependent; $10 minimum. No payment cap. Qualifies for PSLF.
        IBR — still available. 10% of discretionary income (AGI minus 150% of the poverty line for your family size), never more than the 10-year standard payment. Often lower than RAP at PA and NP incomes. Qualifies for PSLF.
        PSLF — intact. 120 qualifying payments at a nonprofit / government employer → 100% tax-free forgiveness. Best deal in health care if your employer qualifies — and most nonprofit hospitals do.
        Non-PSLF IDR forgiveness — now taxable. Carrying loans 20–25 years on IBR/RAP without PSLF now triggers a taxable forgiveness event. Even on a PA or NP balance, that can mean a five-figure tax bill in your 50s or 60s. The old "low payment, wait it out" strategy is now a trap.
        Rates 2026–27: 8.07% for graduate unsubsidized loans, 9.07% for Grad PLUS (fixed at disbursement). Grad PLUS is closed to new borrowers after July 1, 2026. Slider defaults to 8.1%.
        New borrowing caps — PA and NP programs. Graduate programs are capped at $20,500 a year ($100,000 lifetime); "professional" programs at $50,000 a year ($200,000). The Education Department first left PA and NP programs off the professional list; a federal judge blocked that in June 2026, the department widened its list, and it has said it will appeal. Confirm your program's cap with your financial aid office. Gaps filled with private loans never qualify for PSLF or income-driven plans.
